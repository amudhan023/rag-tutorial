Build RAG From Scratch — No Libraries, No Magic

The big picture, before any details

What this whole notebook is building, in one breath.

Problem
An on-call engineer gets paged at 3am: "payment service is down." They don't want to read five 2-page troubleshooting documents. They want to type a question and get the right two paragraphs, instantly.
Why
A general-purpose AI model (like ChatGPT) doesn't know your company's private runbooks — it was never trained on them. If you ask it "how do I restart the payment service," it will either say "I don't know" or, worse, confidently make something up.
What
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is a two-step trick: first retrieve the most relevant pieces of your own documents, then hand those pieces to the AI model and say "answer using only this." The model isn't guessing from memory — it's reading an open book you handed it.
How
This notebook builds that pipeline step by step, showing every intermediate result, using the simplest possible version of each piece.

Why start with something so basic (TF-IDF) instead of a "real" AI model?

TF-IDF turns each chunk of text into a list of numbers where you can literally read what each number means — "this chunk scores 0.72 on the word kafka." A modern neural embedding is a black box: 384 numbers with no individual meaning. Learning the pipeline on the "glass box" version first means when something breaks later, you'll already understand every moving part — you're just swapping one black box in for a see-through one.

Step 1 — Give the system something to search

Overview
Section 1Creating five fake troubleshooting guides
Problem
Before you can search anything, you need something to search. Downloading real company documents would be messy and you wouldn't know exactly what's inside them — which makes it hard to tell later why search got something wrong.
Why
The notebook writes 5 fake "runbooks" (documents like: Payment Service Outage, Order Service Degradation, Login Service Authentication Failures, Kafka Cluster Incidents, Kubernetes Deployment Failures) by hand — so you know every single word in your search corpus, on purpose. That way, when a search result looks wrong later, you can immediately tell if the system is broken or the data just doesn't say that.
What
A corpus is just the fancy word for "the full collection of documents you're going to search." Here it's 5 short text documents, each with a realistic messy shape: a repeated CONFIDENTIAL stamp, page numbers, and troubleshooting steps that include copy-pasted terminal commands.

The code stores the 5 documents in a simple lookup table (name → text), then counts words per document and draws a bar chart, plus prints a little text "card" for each one so it feels like a real, distinct item instead of one giant wall of text.
Plain English

Think of this like setting up a filing cabinet with 5 labeled folders before you build a filing assistant. You wrote all 5 folders yourself, so you know exactly what's inside each one — which makes it obvious later whether your assistant is smart or just lucky.

Step 2 — Cleaning: throwing away the noise

Section 2Removing boilerplate before it pollutes the search
Problem
Every single one of the 5 documents repeats the exact phrase CONFIDENTIAL - ACME Corp Internal and page numbers like Page 1 of 2.
Why
Later, the system will judge "how similar" two chunks of text are. If every single chunk contains the exact same stamp, that stamp makes all documents look a little more alike than they really are — blurring the very thing you're trying to measure. It carries zero useful signal about what a document is actually about, and it actively muddies the comparison.
What
Cleaning means stripping out text that's just administrative noise — headers, footers, page numbers, extra blank lines — before anything gets measured or compared.
How
The code defines a short list of "patterns to delete" (anything starting with "CONFIDENTIAL", anything matching "Page <number> of <number>"), scans every document for text matching those patterns and removes it, then tidies up the double-spaces and extra blank lines that deleting text leaves behind.
Plain English

Imagine photocopying five different reports, but every single page has the same watermark stamped across it. If you tried to judge "which two reports are most alike" by counting shared ink, the watermark would make every pair look artificially similar. Cleaning is peeling that watermark off before you compare anything.

If you skip this cleaning step entirely and re-run the search later, every document's "similarity score" creeps up slightly — for the wrong reason. Noise doesn't make search smarter, it just inflates everyone's score equally, which makes the real signal harder to spot.

Step 3 — Chunking: cutting documents into bite-sized pieces

Section 3Why we don't search whole documents
Problem
If you ask "how do I restart the payment service," and the system compares your question against whole documents, it will match the entire payment-service runbook — including its unrelated Redis and escalation sections that have nothing to do with restarting anything.
Why
Two reasons this matters. First, precision: smaller pieces give sharper, more specific matches. Second, budget: the AI model you eventually hand text to has a strict limit on how much text it can read at once (and it costs real money per word) — you want to send only the 2–3 most relevant paragraphs, not five entire documents.
What
Chunking means slicing every document into small overlapping windows of text — say, 60 words at a time — so the system can retrieve just the one small piece that actually answers the question.
How
The code slides a fixed-size "window" (e.g. 60 words) across each document, moving forward a bit less than the full window size each step — which is what creates the overlap explained next. Each resulting window of words becomes one "chunk."
Plain English

Chunking is like cutting a long recipe book into individual recipe cards instead of forcing someone to flip through the whole book to find "how do I make bread." Smaller cards mean whoever is searching gets handed exactly the one card they need.

The core trade-off, made visible:

Chunks too smallprecise matches, but commands/context get cut in half
⇄
Chunks too largefull context, but fuzzy matches and fat prompts
Section 3.1Watching a command get amputated at a chunk boundary
Problem
A command like kubectl rollout restart deployment payment-api might happen to sit exactly at the border between chunk 3 and chunk 4. If chunks don't overlap at all, the first half of that command ends up in chunk 3 and the second half in chunk 4 — and neither chunk contains the complete, useful command.
Why
A search for "how do I restart payments?" needs to find a chunk holding the whole command. If the command is split across two chunks, neither chunk is a good match — the answer effectively disappears, even though it was right there in the source document.
What
Overlap means each new chunk repeats the last several words of the previous chunk. So the "boundary region" — wherever a command happened to land — always ends up living fully inside at least one chunk.
How
The notebook runs the exact same document through the chunker twice: once with 0 words of overlap, once with 10 words of overlap — changing only that one setting — and prints the boundary of each chunk so you can watch the command survive intact only in the second version.
Example — the same boundary, two ways

Command: kubectl rollout restart deployment payment-api

chunk 3 ends: ...pods in CrashLoopBackOff, restart the chunk 4 begins: deployment payment-api If Redis...

↑ overlap = 0 — the command is split. No single chunk contains it.

chunk 3 ends: ...restart the deployment: kubectl rollout restart deployment payment-api chunk 4 begins: kubectl rollout restart deployment payment-api If Redis...

↑ overlap = 10 words — the full command now appears intact in both neighboring chunks.

Trade-off — overlap isn't free

Repeating words duplicates data. A 10-word overlap on a 40-word chunk is 25% storage overhead — in a real production system that means real disk space and real embedding compute cost for every document. Typical real-world values sit around 10–20% of the chunk size, not 25%. This notebook settles on chunk size = 60 words, overlap = 12 words for everything that follows — a fresh set of chunks that also now carries metadata (which document each chunk came from, and a unique ID) so a result can always be traced back to its source runbook.

Step 4 — Turning text into numbers a computer can compare

Section 4TF-IDF: an embedding you can actually read
Problem
Computers can't compare "meaning" directly. To ask "which chunk is most similar to this question," you first need to turn every piece of text into numbers — because comparing numbers is something math can do.
Why
The entire idea behind every search and AI-retrieval system boils down to one sentence: turn text into numbers such that similar meaning produces nearby numbers. Get that translation right, and "search" becomes "find the nearest numbers" — a problem math is very good at solving.
What
TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency) is the simplest useful way to do that translation. In one breath: a word that shows up often in this one chunk matters to that chunk (Term Frequency); a word that shows up in almost every chunk — like "the," "check," "service" — carries no useful signal, so it gets down-weighted (Inverse Document Frequency). Multiply those two together, and you get one score per word, per chunk. Every chunk becomes a long list of numbers — one number per distinct word across the whole corpus — called a vector.
How
The code scans every chunk once to build a "vocabulary" — every distinct word used anywhere — dropping common filler words like "the" and "and." Then it scores each chunk against that vocabulary, producing one row of numbers per chunk. With roughly 300 distinct words across the corpus and only 10 chunks, almost every chunk uses just a handful of those 300 words — so the vast majority of each chunk's number-list is exactly zero.
Plain English

Picture a giant spreadsheet. Every column is a distinct word that appears anywhere in any runbook — "kafka," "kubectl," "redis," "login," and so on. Every row is one chunk of text. Each cell in the spreadsheet says "how much does this word matter to this chunk?" A chunk about Kafka lights up strongly in the "kafka," "broker," and "partition" columns and stays at zero everywhere else. That row of numbers — the chunk's entire spreadsheet row — is its vector.

The one property that makes TF-IDF special

Because there's exactly one column per word, you can point at any single number in a chunk's vector and say precisely which word it came from. A modern neural embedding (used in Notebook 2) compresses everything into ~384 numbers that don't correspond to individual words at all — it's a black box that understands synonyms, but you can't point at "number 213" and say what it means. This notebook trades that power away on purpose, so you can watch the mechanism work in full daylight first.

Step 5 — Drawing a map of the "meaning space"

Section 5Squashing 300 dimensions down to 2, just so we can look at it
Problem
Each chunk's vector lives in a space with ~300 dimensions (one per vocabulary word). Humans can't visualize 300 dimensions.
Why
Seeing the actual "shape" of how chunks relate to each other — which ones cluster together, which sit far apart — builds real intuition for what "similarity" means before you trust a similarity score as just a number.
What
PCA (Principal Component Analysis) finds the 2 directions, out of all ~300, along which the chunks vary the most, and projects every chunk's vector down onto just those 2 — like taking a flattering photograph of a 3D object from exactly the angle that shows its shape best. It's a lossy compression, done purely so a human can look at a 2D scatter plot on a screen.
How
Each chunk becomes one dot with an (x, y) position; dots are colored by which runbook they came from; the notebook plots all 10 dots on one chart.
Plain English

Think of PCA like flattening a globe into a 2D map. You lose some accuracy at the edges, but you gain the ability to actually look at the whole world at once. Here, PCA flattens 300-number fingerprints into simple (x, y) dots you can eyeball on a chart.

A simplified sketch of what the scatter plot shows:

● ● ● payment-service ● ● order-service ● ● login-service

↑ these three cluster near each other — they all share "kubectl," "pods," "restart," "deployment"

● ● ● kafka-cluster

↑ sits apart — different tooling vocabulary ("broker," "partitions," "lag")

A hazard this map foreshadows

The kubernetes-deploys runbook lands near the three service runbooks on this map, because they all talk about kubectl. That's a preview of a real problem: shared vocabulary creates false neighbors. Two chunks can look similar to a word-counting method just because they both mention the same tool, even when they're about completely different incidents. Fixing this is exactly the job of "re-ranking," covered in Notebook 2.

Step 6 — The "vector database" is just a list

Section 6Demystifying what a vector database actually does
Problem
Terms like "vector database," "pgvector," "FAISS," and "Pinecone" sound intimidating and complicated, as if they're doing something fundamentally magical.
What
Underneath, any vector database does exactly two jobs: (1) store pairs of (a vector, some descriptive info about it) and (2) given a new vector, return the closest matches. That's it. This notebook's "database" is a plain Python list of dictionaries — and its "search engine" is a for-loop that checks every item. That isn't a toy simplification; it's the literal algorithm (called brute-force nearest-neighbor search) that fancy databases only add speed tricks on top of, once you have millions of items instead of ten.
How
Every chunk's vector, plus its source document name and chunk ID, gets stored together as one record in a list. Then a hand-written function computes cosine similarity — a score from -1 to 1 measuring how closely two vectors "point in the same direction," regardless of their length — between any two vectors.
Plain English — what is "cosine similarity"?

Imagine two arrows drawn from the same starting point. If they point in almost exactly the same direction, the angle between them is small — "very similar." If they point in completely different directions, the angle is large — "not similar." Cosine similarity measures that angle and turns it into a single number: close to 1 means "pointing the same way" (very similar), close to 0 means "unrelated." Crucially, it ignores how long each arrow is — so a short chunk and a long chunk about the exact same topic can still score close to 1.

Sanity check the notebook runs before trusting the metric
cosine(kafka chunk, another kafka chunk)≈ 0.30 — same topic, meaningfully higher
cosine(kafka chunk, login chunk)≈ 0.02 — different topic, near zero

Step 7 — Actually searching: the most important step in the notebook

Section 7Putting it all together into one search function
Problem
Everything so far has been preparation. Now: given a real question typed by a human, actually find the best-matching chunks.
What
The full search algorithm, in four steps: turn the question into a vector using the exact same method used for the chunks → compute cosine similarity between the question's vector and every chunk's vector → sort all chunks from most similar to least → keep only the top few.
How
The question gets converted into a vector using the vocabulary that was already learned from the documents — not a fresh vocabulary. Any word in the question that never appeared anywhere in the runbooks is simply ignored, because there's no column for it. Then the code loops through every stored chunk, computes its similarity to the question, and returns the highest-scoring handful.
One critical rule, and a real production bug it prevents

The question must be turned into a vector using the exact same vocabulary that scored the documents. If a teammate accidentally embeds new questions with a different method than the one used to build the stored chunks, the two sets of numbers live in unrelated number-spaces — comparing them produces meaningless scores that still look like normal output (no error message!), just quietly wrong. This exact bug happens for real when someone upgrades an embedding model without re-processing the whole document collection.

Section 7.1Probing where this simple method breaks — memorize these two
Problem
Search works great when the question happens to share exact words with the right document. Real on-call engineers don't always phrase things that conveniently.
Failure 1 — Synonym blindness

The query "The message bus is falling behind" means exactly the same thing to any SRE as "Kafka consumer lag is increasing." A human reads it instantly. But it scores ≈ 0.000 against the Kafka runbook, because it shares zero exact words with it — no "kafka," no "consumer," no "lag" anywhere in the query. TF-IDF only ever matches literal strings, never meaning. This is the single biggest limitation this whole notebook is building up to expose — and Notebook 2's neural embeddings fix precisely this.

Failure 2 — Vocabulary collision

The query "Customers see failures at checkout" is obviously about payments. But it retrieves the Kubernetes runbook instead, because the word "failures" happens to be a strong, distinctive word inside that document ("pods stuck in ImagePullBackOff/CrashLoopBackOff," "rollout stuck," etc.), while the payment-service runbook never actually uses the word "failures" at all. The system matched the right word, in completely the wrong context. Fixing this specific kind of mistake is the job of "re-ranking," covered in Notebook 2.

Step 8 — Building the message you'll actually send to an AI model

Section 8Pasting retrieved text into a prompt
Problem
You've now found the right chunks. But an AI language model doesn't automatically know to use them — you have to hand them over explicitly, in the message ("prompt") you send it.
Why
RAG's whole trick, once you strip away the buzzwords, is almost embarrassingly simple: paste the retrieved text straight into the prompt. Most people using RAG systems never actually look at the assembled prompt — but you should, because inspecting the exact prompt is the #1 debugging tool: it instantly answers "did the AI make something up, or did retrieval hand it the wrong information in the first place?"
What
A prompt template with three real production design decisions baked in: each retrieved chunk is labeled with exactly which document it came from (enabling citations later), the instructions explicitly demand the model answer only from the provided text (reducing made-up answers), and there's an explicit permission to say "I don't know" — because a model needs to be told it's allowed to refuse, or it will often guess anyway.
How
The code re-runs the search from Step 7, stitches the top few chunks together — each one tagged with its source ID and similarity score — into one block of "context" text, and wraps it in instructions plus the original question.
Example — a real assembled prompt (simplified)
Instruction"Answer using ONLY the context below. Cite the source chunk id. If the context doesn't contain the answer, say so — don't guess."
Context[Source: payment-service#0 | similarity=0.44] "...restart the deployment: kubectl rollout restart deployment payment-api..."
Question"How do I restart the payment service?"
Every retrieved chunk has a cost

Real AI APIs charge per "token" (a word-ish unit of text), and take longer to respond the more text you send. Retrieving k=3 chunks versus k=8 chunks isn't just a quality knob — it's directly a cost and speed knob too. This trade-off shows up constantly in real production systems as a place to save money without hurting answer quality much.

Step 9 — Getting an actual answer back

Section 9A free "fake AI" that proves the whole pipeline works end to end
Problem
Sending the prompt to a real AI model (like GPT) normally costs money and requires an API key — a real barrier if you just want to see the whole pipeline run once.
What
A mock LLM — a stand-in fake that doesn't understand language at all, but mechanically pulls out anything that looks like a terminal command (starting with kubectl, redis-cli, etc.) from the retrieved context and lists it as the "answer." It proves every earlier stage — clean, chunk, embed, search, build prompt — worked correctly, completely free.
How
A pattern-matching rule scans the context text for command-shaped text and lists whatever it finds; if it finds nothing, it replies "I don't have a runbook for that." A real AI model can be swapped in later by uncommenting a few lines and pasting in an API key — nothing else about the pipeline changes.
A real bug the notebook authors hit while building this

Their word-based chunker joined words back together with plain spaces, which quietly destroyed line breaks — so trying to extract "command lines" line-by-line found nothing at all, even though the commands were still there as plain text. The fix was matching commands by their shape (regex) instead of relying on line breaks. The broader lesson: chunking can silently throw away a document's structure (line breaks, tables, code blocks) in ways that don't show up as an error — they show up as a feature quietly not working.

Step 10 — The experiment that reveals the real bottleneck

Section 10Turning the chunking knobs and measuring what actually changes
Problem
Does making chunks bigger or smaller, or adding more overlap, actually fix the two failures from Section 7.1?
Why
This is the notebook's core teaching moment. Teams building real retrieval systems commonly spend weeks tweaking chunk size when the real problem lies somewhere entirely different — a systematic way to measure "which knob actually matters" saves that wasted effort.
What
A small test set of 10 questions with known correct answers, deliberately split into two halves: 5 "easy" questions that share exact vocabulary with their answer, and 5 "hard" paraphrased questions that mean the same thing but use different words. For each combination of chunk size and overlap, the notebook rebuilds the whole search index from scratch and measures hit@1 — "for what fraction of test questions was the single top search result actually correct?"
How
It sweeps chunk size across 20, 40, 60, 100, and 200 words, at both 0% and 20% overlap — 10 total configurations — rebuilding the index and re-scoring all 10 questions each time, then plots hit@1 against chunk size as a line chart.

Hit@1 sits at roughly 0.5 no matter what chunk size or overlap you choose. The 5 "easy" (lexical) questions succeed every time; the 5 "paraphrased" (semantic) questions fail every time.
